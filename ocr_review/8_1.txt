THERE are prisons, into which whoever looks will, at first sight
of the people confined, be convinced, that there is some great
or in the management of them: their sallow meagre coun-
enances declare, without words, that they are very miserable.
Many who went in healthy, are in a few months changed to
emaciated dejected objects. Some are seen pining under
diseases, “sick, and in prison”; expiring on the floors, in loath-
some cells, of pestilential fevers, and the confluent smallpox;
victims, I must not say to the cruelty, but I will say to the

There are several bridewells (to begin with them) in which
isoners have no allowance of food at all. In some, the keeper
ms what little is allowed them: and where he engages to supply
each prisoner with one or two pennyworth of bread a day, I
ave known this shrunk to half, sometimes less than half the
quantity, cut or broken from his own loaf.
It will perhaps be asked, does not their work maintain them?
for every one knows that those offenders are committed to hard
bour. The answer to that question, though true, will hardly
believed. There are few bridewells in which any work is
done, or can be done. The prisoners have neither tools, nor
terials of any kind: but spend their time in sloth, profaneness
d debauchery, to a degree which, in some of those houses
that I have seen, is extremely shocking.
Some keepers of these houses, who have represented to the
gistrates the wants of their prisoners, and desired for them
cessary food, have been silenced with these inconsiderate
ords, Let them work or starve. When those gentlemen know
former is impossible, do they not by that thoughtless
tence, inevitably doom poor creatures to the latter?
